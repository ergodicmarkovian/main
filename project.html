Receiver-Usable Communication in Multi-Agent AI: More information does not always lead to better decisions. Even a correct message can fail if the receiving agent cannot easily use it. When should agents exchange short messages, share the full context, or work independently? We study communication through its effect on the receiver's next decision, accounting for both useful information and the burden of processing it.
Technical contributions:
  • A receiver-relative theory of communication that explains why Bayes-sufficient compression can still fail for agents with bounded processing capabilities.
  • A decomposition into externalization, absorption, and action closure to distinguish missing information from failures to turn recovered information into the correct action.
  • An inference-time protocol selector, evaluated across six benchmark families, that balances receiver gain, communication burden, and cost.

Stable Training and Plug-and-Play Multi-Agent LLMs: Can a team of smaller language models compete with a much larger model? Training such teams is challenging: updating one agent changes the context seen by the others, potentially disrupting coordination. Through Sequential Agent Tuning (SAT) and TeamTR, we develop sequential training methods that control these changes and support upgrading individual agents without retraining the entire team.
Technical contributions:
  • SAT: coordinator-free sequential agent tuning with sequence-aware advantage estimation and per-agent KL trust regions, providing improvement bounds and plug-and-play certificates under the stated assumptions.
  • TeamTR: an analysis of compounding occupancy shift showing that, under the paper's trust-region assumptions, fresh intermediate-policy evaluation reduces the stale-occupancy penalty's dependence on team size from quadratic to linear.
  • Practical sequential fine-tuning with fresh rollouts and controlled agent updates, evaluated on reasoning and planning tasks and component upgrades.

Counterfactual Prediction and Control: Humans and robots learn to perceive and interact with the world via real-time interactions. Can they do this not by real-time interactions but by learning only from past experiences? This is challenging since the past experience may be very different from the current scenario, which is termed "counterfactual."
Technical contributions:
  • Off-policy stable prediction algorithms in sequential decision-making and their sample complexity analysis.
  • Off-policy policy gradient theorem with function approximation and counterfactual density ratio estimation.
